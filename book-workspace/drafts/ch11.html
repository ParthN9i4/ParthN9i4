Chapter 10 built a network and asserted that its gradient could be computed. This chapter discharges the assertion. The algorithm is not a neural-network algorithm: it is reverse-mode automatic differentiation, reinvented under at least four names since the 1960s, and its entire content is one asymptotic fact. For $f:\mathbb{R}^N\to\mathbb{R}$ evaluated by a straight-line program of $T$ arithmetic operations, the complete gradient $\nabla f$ — all $N$ partial derivatives, exact to roundoff — costs $O(T)$ operations with a small constant and no dependence on $N$. Every other method costs $\Omega(NT)$. That single fact is why a $10^{11}$-parameter model can be trained at all. The price is memory: reverse mode must remember the forward pass, which is why Section 11.5 exists and why Chapters 30 and 32 spend so much of their budget on activation storage. Sections 11.1–11.3 derive the algorithm, 11.4 implements it, and 11.7 explains what PyTorch and JAX actually do differently.

11.1 The Chain Rule as a Graph Operation

A program that computes $f$ is already a factorization of $f$ into primitives whose derivatives are known in closed form. Differentiation is then a graph traversal, and the only design decision is which direction to traverse.

Definition 11.1 (Evaluation trace)

Fix an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The evaluation trace of a program is a finite sequence $v_1,\ldots,v_T$ in which $v_i = x_i$ for $i \le n$ and, for $i > n$, $v_i = \varphi_i\big(v_j : j \in \mathrm{Pa}(i)\big)$ where each $\varphi_i$ is a primitive with a known derivative and $\mathrm{Pa}(i) \subset \{1,\ldots,i-1\}$. The outputs are the last $m$ entries. The trace is a directed acyclic graph with $T$ nodes; because $\mathrm{Pa}(i) \subset \{1,\ldots,i-1\}$, index order is a topological order. Every conditional, loop, and function call is already resolved in the trace: the trace records the branch taken at $x$, not the program text.

Symbolic differentiation manipulates the expression rather than the trace. Applied to $u = \prod_{i=1}^{k} g_i(x)$, the product rule returns $k$ terms, each a product of $k$ factors; nesting this through $L$ composed layers produces an expression whose size grows exponentially in $L$ unless shared subexpressions are explicitly detected and reused. Once you add that reuse you have rebuilt automatic differentiation, which is the correct way to see AD: it is symbolic differentiation with aggressive common-subexpression elimination, evaluated numerically as it goes.

Numerical differentiation perturbs. The central difference $\partial_i f \approx [f(x+\epsilon e_i) - f(x-\epsilon e_i)]/2\epsilon$ carries truncation error $\tfrac{1}{6}|f'''|\epsilon^2$ and, because the two evaluations agree to leading order and cancel catastrophically, roundoff error of order $u|f|/\epsilon$ with $u = 2.22\times10^{-16}$ the double-precision unit roundoff (Chapter 5). Minimizing the sum gives $\epsilon^\star \propto u^{1/3} \approx 6.1\times10^{-6}$ and an error floor of order $u^{2/3} \approx 3.7\times10^{-11}$ — five significant digits lost, irreducibly, no matter how the step is tuned. Certain Worse, it costs $2N$ evaluations of $f$ for a full gradient. At $N = 10^{11}$, one gradient step would require $2\times10^{11}$ forward passes.

11.2 Forward Mode (JVP)

Carry a derivative alongside every value. Formally, augment each $v_i$ with a tangent $\dot v_i = \partial v_i/\partial s$ for a scalar path parameter $s$, seeded by $\dot x = v \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

Definition 11.2 (Jacobian-vector product, forward mode)

For $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ with Jacobian $J(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, the JVP is $v \mapsto J(x)\,v$. Forward-mode AD evaluates it by propagating tangents in trace order, $$\dot v_i = \sum_{j \in \mathrm{Pa}(i)} \frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial v_j}\,\dot v_j ,$$ with $\dot v_j = v_j$-component of the seed for inputs. Equivalently, evaluate the program in the dual numbers $\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$: replace $x$ by $x + \varepsilon v$ and read the $\varepsilon$-coefficient of the output, which is $J v$ exactly because $\varphi(a+\varepsilon b) = \varphi(a) + \varepsilon\,\varphi'(a)b$ truncates at first order.

Theorem 11.1 (Forward-mode cost)

Let $T$ be the number of primitive operations in the trace, each with at most $c$ arguments. One JVP costs at most $(1+2c)\,T$ operations and $O(1)$ extra memory beyond the forward pass. Obtaining the full Jacobian requires $n$ JVPs, one per input coordinate: total cost $\Theta(nT)$.

Proof. Each node contributes its own evaluation plus at most $c$ partial derivatives and $c$ multiply-adds, so the augmented trace has at most $(1+2c)T$ operations. Tangents are consumed in the same order they are produced, so $\dot v_i$ can overwrite storage as soon as its consumers have run — forward mode needs no tape. Recovering column $j$ of $J$ requires the seed $v = e_j$, and the map $v \mapsto Jv$ is linear, so $n$ independent seeds are necessary to determine $J$ in the absence of structural assumptions. $\blacksquare$

Forward mode is the right choice when $n \ll m$: one input, many outputs. It is also the mode that composes trivially with itself for higher derivatives, and the mode used inside a Hessian-vector product (Section 11.6). For a loss function, $m = 1$ and $n = N = 10^{11}$, which is the worst possible case — $10^{11}$ passes.

11.3 Reverse Mode (VJP) and the Tape

Reverse mode propagates sensitivities of one output backwards to all inputs. Write $\bar v_i = \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial v_i$ for the adjoint (or cotangent) of node $i$, where $\mathcal{L}$ is the scalar output.

Definition 11.3 (Vector-Jacobian product, reverse mode)

The VJP is $w \mapsto w^\top J(x)$, equivalently $J(x)^\top w$. Reverse-mode AD evaluates it by seeding $\bar v_T = 1$ (for scalar output) and sweeping the trace in reverse index order, $$\bar v_j = \sum_{i \,:\, j \in \mathrm{Pa}(i)} \bar v_i \, \frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial v_j} .$$ The sum over consumers $i$ is the multivariate chain rule and is the reason a node used twice receives the sum of two contributions. Backpropagation is precisely this sweep applied to a feedforward network; nothing about it is specific to neural networks.

Theorem 11.2 (Cheap gradient principle)

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ be computed by a trace of $T$ primitives, each with at most $c$ arguments. Then the full gradient $\nabla f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is computed by one forward pass plus one reverse sweep, at total cost at most $(2+2c)\,T$ operations — independent of $n$ — and memory $O(T)$.

Proof. The forward pass costs $T$. In the reverse sweep each node $i$ is visited once and performs at most $c$ partial-derivative evaluations and $c$ multiply-accumulates into its parents' adjoints, so the sweep costs at most $(1+2c)T$. Every edge of the DAG is traversed exactly once in each direction, and no quantity depends on $n$ except the final answer's length. Correctness is induction on reverse index order: when node $i$ is visited, all consumers of $i$ have larger index and have already been visited, so $\bar v_i$ has received every term of the chain-rule sum. Memory: each $\bar v_j$ needs $\partial\varphi_i/\partial v_j$, which generally depends on the forward values, so the forward values (or enough to recompute them) must be retained — $O(T)$ in the worst case. $\blacksquare$

Baur and Strassen proved the sharp form of this in 1983 for rational functions: all $n$ partials cost at most a small constant times the cost of the function itself. Certain In deep learning the constant is concrete. A linear layer's forward pass is one matmul $XW$; its backward pass is two, $\bar X = \bar Z W^\top$ and $\bar W = X^\top \bar Z$. Backward therefore costs about $2\times$ forward and a training step about $3\times$ — the origin of the $C \approx 6ND$ FLOP rule that Chapter 24 uses. Measured on the engine below (an 8-layer, width-256 MLP, batch 256, pure NumPy), forward is 8.04 ms and forward-plus-backward is 25.37 ms, a ratio of $3.15$. Verify

The asymmetry is structural: a JVP needs only values already available going forward, while a VJP needs local Jacobians of operations that have already run, so their inputs must be kept. Section 11.5 renegotiates that trade.

11.4 Building an Engine: Tensor, Broadcasting, Matmul

An engine needs three things: a record of the trace (the tape), a VJP rule per primitive, and a correct reverse traversal. The tape can be implicit. If every node stores its parents and a construction timestamp, then sorting reachable nodes by descending timestamp is a reverse topological order, because a node can only be constructed after its parents exist. That is the whole of the graph machinery.

PrimitiveForwardVJP: given $\bar z$, return
add$z = a + b$$\bar a = \rho_a(\bar z)$, $\bar b = \rho_b(\bar z)$
mul$z = a \odot b$$\bar a = \rho_a(\bar z \odot b)$, $\bar b = \rho_b(\bar z \odot a)$
matmul$Z = AB$$\bar A = \bar Z B^\top$, $\bar B = A^\top \bar Z$
relu$z = \max(a,0)$$\bar a = \bar z \odot \mathbb{1}[a > 0]$

log$z = \log a$$\bar a = \bar z / a$
sum$z = \sum_{\text{axis}} a$$\bar a = \mathrm{broadcast}(\bar z)$

Here $\rho_s$ denotes reduction back to shape $s$, defined below. The matmul rule follows from indices: with $Z_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}$ and $\bar A_{ik} = \sum_{ij'} \bar Z_{ij'} \,\partial Z_{ij'}/\partial A_{ik} = \sum_j \bar Z_{ij} B_{kj} = (\bar Z B^\top)_{ik}$, and symmetrically for $\bar B$. Note the pleasing duality of the last row: summation and broadcasting are transposes of each other, which is the key to the next paragraph.

Broadcasting is a linear map. Adding a bias of shape $(1,d)$ to activations of shape $(B,d)$ applies a matrix of zeros and ones that copies the bias $B$ times. The transpose of a copy is a sum. So the gradient arriving at the bias, which has shape $(B,d)$, must be summed over the batch axis to shape $(1,d)$ before it can be an element of the bias's own space. Stating it as a rule: after computing a VJP under broadcasting, sum the result over every axis that was prepended and every axis that was stretched from length 1, keeping dimensions in the latter case.

Example 11.1 (Broadcast reduction, by hand)

Let $v = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\3&4&5\end{pmatrix}$ and $c = \begin{pmatrix}10&20&30\end{pmatrix}$ of shape $(1,3)$, and $\mathcal{L} = \sum_{ij} (v \odot c)_{ij}$. Forward: $\mathcal{L} = (0{+}20{+}60) + (30{+}80{+}150) = 340$. The upstream gradient of the sum is a $(2,3)$ matrix of ones; the mul rule gives $\bar c = \mathbf{1}\odot v$ of shape $(2,3)$, which is not the shape of $c$. Reducing over the stretched axis gives $\bar c = (0{+}3,\;1{+}4,\;2{+}5) = (3,5,7)$, which is exactly $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial c_j = \sum_i v_{ij}$. The engine prints dc = [3. 5. 7.]. Note that $\bar v = c$ broadcast back to $(2,3)$ needs no reduction — only the operand that was broadcast gets reduced.

Common Pitfall

Omitting the broadcast reduction is the single most common bug in a hand-written engine, and its worst form is silent. If parameters are stored with shape $(d,)$ and the update is written p.data -= lr * p.grad with a $(B,d)$ gradient, NumPy raises a broadcast error on the in-place subtraction and you find the bug in a minute. Write the same update out of place, p.data = p.data - lr * p.grad, and NumPy happily broadcasts: the parameter silently becomes shape $(B,d)$, every subsequent forward pass still runs, the loss decreases for a while, and the model now has $B$ independent copies of each bias that no longer correspond to anything. A second silent variant: if $B$ happens to equal 1 during a debug run, the shapes agree and the bug hides until the first real batch. Test with $B \ne 1$ and assert p.grad.shape == p.data.shape at every step.

11.4.1 Non-differentiable points and in-place mutation

ReLU is not differentiable at $0$, and neither is $|x|$, $\max$, clipping, or any quantizer. The Clarke subdifferential at that point is the interval $\partial\,\mathrm{relu}(0) = [0,1]$, and every engine simply picks an element: PyTorch 2.13 returns $0$ for both $\mathrm{relu}'(0)$ and $|x|'(0)$, and the artifact below matches that convention by using the mask $\mathbb{1}[a > 0]$ rather than $\mathbb{1}[a \ge 0]$. Verify The usual defense is that the bad set has measure zero, so with continuously distributed inputs it is hit with probability zero.

That defense is not quite true, and the failure is worth seeing. Consider $f(x) = \mathrm{relu}(x) - \mathrm{relu}(-x)$. This equals $x$ for every real $x$, so $f$ is smooth with $f' \equiv 1$. Yet AD evaluates $f'(0) = \mathrm{relu}'(0) - (-1)\cdot\mathrm{relu}'(0) = 0$. Both PyTorch and the engine below return $0.0$ where the true derivative is $1.0$. AD therefore returns a wrong derivative at a point where the composite function is differentiable; the guarantee is only about the piecewise structure, not about $f$. Contested Bolte and Pauwels showed that for programs built from semialgebraic pieces, AD returns a Clarke subgradient almost everywhere and that stochastic gradient descent using it converges to Clarke critical points; the opposing view is that exact zeros are not measure-zero events in real pipelines, because padding, masks, dropout, and ReLU-after-ReLU produce exact zeros constantly. My position: the subgradient choice at zero has never been shown to change training outcomes measurably, and the practical hazards in this family are the ones that produce $\infty$ or NaN — $\sqrt{x}$ at $0$ has derivative $\infty$ (PyTorch returns inf, verified), $\log(0)$ likewise — because a single NaN adjoint contaminates every upstream parameter in one sweep. The evidence that would settle it is a controlled experiment varying only the convention in $\{0, \tfrac12, 1\}$ across seeds; to my knowledge nobody has published one at scale.

In-place mutation is the other correctness trap. If $z = e^a$ is overwritten after the fact, the exp VJP $\bar a = \bar z \odot z$ silently uses the wrong $z$. PyTorch defends with a version counter on every tensor's storage and refuses at backward time: "one of the variables needed for gradient computation has been modified by an inplace operation … is at version 1; expected version 0". The engine below sidesteps the issue by never mutating .data inside an operation; the optimizer does mutate, but only after backward() has consumed the tape.

11.5 Activation Checkpointing

Theorem 11.2's memory term is the binding constraint at scale. For an $L$-layer network the forward pass stores $\Theta(L)$ activation tensors, each of size $\Theta(B \cdot d)$, and for a transformer these dominate parameter and optimizer memory by a wide margin at large batch. Checkpointing buys memory with recomputation: store a subset of activations, discard the rest, and regenerate what is needed by re-running forward from the nearest stored checkpoint.

Theorem 11.3 (Square-root checkpointing schedule)

Consider a chain of $L$ equal-cost layers. Store every $s$-th activation, keeping $k = L/s$ checkpoints, and recompute segments on demand during the reverse sweep. Peak activation memory is $\Theta(k + s)$ and total compute is at most $2\times$ the un-checkpointed forward plus the backward. Minimizing $k + s = L/s + s$ over $s$ gives $s^\star = \sqrt{L}$, hence $k^\star = \sqrt{L}$ and peak memory $\Theta(\sqrt{L})$ at the cost of exactly one extra forward pass.

Proof. Peak memory is $k$ checkpoints held for the whole sweep plus, at any moment, the $s$ activations of the single segment currently being recomputed and backpropagated: $k+s = L/s + s$. This is convex in $s$ with derivative $1 - L/s^2$, zero at $s=\sqrt{L}$, giving $2\sqrt{L}$. Each segment is recomputed exactly once, so recomputation costs one additional forward pass over the whole chain. With backward at $2\times$ forward, the step goes from $3\times$ to $4\times$ forward cost: a $33\%$ compute overhead. $\blacksquare$

Two refinements matter. First, $\Theta(\sqrt L)$ is not optimal: Griewank and Walther's revolve algorithm achieves $O(\log L)$ memory with $O(\log L)$ recomputations per layer and is optimal for a chain under a fixed budget, yet deep learning nearly always uses $\sqrt{L}$ or a hand-chosen per-block schedule, because constant factors and graph irregularity eat the asymptotic win. Likely Second, the right granularity is not "every layer" but "expensive to store, cheap to recompute": softmax and activation outputs are large and nearly free to regenerate, while a matmul output should be kept. Chapter 31 shows FlashAttention as the extreme case, never writing the attention matrix at all.

11.6 Higher-Order Derivatives and Double Backward

The gradient is itself computed by a program, so it can be differentiated by the same machinery — provided the reverse sweep is traced. This is the distinction PyTorch exposes as create_graph=True: by default the backward pass runs outside the tape and produces plain numbers; with the flag set, each VJP is itself recorded, and $\nabla f$ becomes a differentiable function. The engine in the artifact writes its VJPs in raw NumPy, so it is deliberately first-order only; making it second-order means rewriting each _bw in terms of Tensor operations, which is a mechanical change of about twenty lines.

The object you almost never want is the Hessian itself: $N^2$ entries is $10^{22}$ at $N = 10^{11}$. The object you do want is the Hessian-vector product $H v$, which costs $O(1)$ gradient evaluations by any of three compositions. Reverse-over-reverse computes $\nabla(\langle\nabla f, v\rangle)$ with $v$ held constant. Forward-over-reverse pushes a tangent through the gradient program, $Hv = \tfrac{d}{d\varepsilon}\nabla f(x+\varepsilon v)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}$, and is usually the cheapest and most numerically stable in practice; it is what jax.jvp(jax.grad(f), ...) does. Finite-differencing the gradient approximates the same thing: on the test function $f(x) = (x^\top x)^2 + \sum_i x_i^3$, whose exact Hessian is $8xx^\top + 4(x^\top x)I + 6\,\mathrm{diag}(x)$, the engine's gradient central-differenced along $v$ matched $Hv$ to $5.8\times10^{-9}$ absolute against $\|Hv\| = 98.7$ — about $6\times10^{-11}$ relative, exactly the $u^{2/3}$ floor from Section 11.1. Certain HVPs are the engine behind Gauss-Newton and K-FAC approximations (Chapter 13), influence functions, and curvature-based sharpness measures (Chapter 14).

11.7 What PyTorch and JAX Actually Do

PyTorch is define-by-run. Each differentiable operation on a tensor with requires_grad=True allocates a C++ Node holding the saved inputs and a pointer to the next nodes; grad_fn is that node. The graph is a value, built fresh each iteration and destroyed after backward() unless retained. Arbitrary Python control flow therefore works with no special support, since only the branch actually taken is recorded; the cost is that nothing is known ahead of time, so eager mode permits no cross-operation optimization. torch.compile answers this by tracing to an FX graph, building a joint forward-backward graph, and running a partitioner that decides which intermediates to save and which to recompute — automating Section 11.5's decision.

JAX is trace-and-transform. jax.grad is not a tape at all: it traces the function to a jaxpr, linearizes it via partial evaluation into a JVP program, then transposes that linear program to obtain the VJP. Because the derivative is a program transformation producing another program, it composes arbitrarily — grad(grad(grad(f))) is unremarkable — and everything is available to XLA for fusion. The cost is that the traced function must be expressible in the traced language: Python-level control flow on traced values must become lax.cond/lax.scan, shapes must be static, and side effects must be threaded explicitly.

Contested Which stance is right is genuinely disputed. The define-by-run camp argues that research velocity dominates and that compilers should adapt to dynamic programs; the trace-and-transform camp argues that only a program representation permits whole-graph optimization, automatic rematerialization, and clean higher-order transforms, and that dynamism is mostly a habit rather than a requirement. The technical trend favors convergence: torch.compile imports the traced-graph benefits into an eager frontend, and JAX has grown escape hatches for dynamism. What would settle it is longitudinal evidence on what fraction of large-scale training runs actually execute compiled graphs and how often compilation is abandoned because of graph breaks — a number nobody publishes. Verify My position: eager-by-default with an opt-in compiler is the correct engineering answer for research and the wrong one for inference serving, where the graph is static and every percent of throughput is money (Chapter 33).

Artifact 11.1 — A Reverse-Mode Autodiff Engine in 236 Lines

A tape-based Tensor supporting +, *, @, relu, exp, log, sum, with correct broadcast reduction in the backward pass, verified three ways: finite differences on random computation graphs, torch.autograd on a 3-layer MLP, and training that MLP on synthetic two-moons. The engine alone is saved as autodiff_engine.py for later chapters to import.

"""Artifact 11.1 -- a tape-based reverse-mode autodiff engine, pure NumPy.
Verified (a) against central finite differences on random computation graphs,
(b) against torch.autograd on a 3-layer MLP, (c) by training that MLP on
synthetic two-moons with nothing but this engine.  torch is optional.
"""
import time
import numpy as np
try:
    import torch
except ImportError:
    torch = None

# Every Tensor takes a monotonically increasing id at construction, so sorting
# by DESCENDING id is a reverse topological order: a node can only be built
# after all of its parents exist.  That ordering is the whole of "the tape".
_COUNTER = [0]

def _unbroadcast(g, shape):
    """Reduce gradient `g` back to `shape`, undoing NumPy broadcasting.
    Broadcasting is a linear map that COPIES entries; its transpose SUMS them.
    Omitting this is the classic autodiff bug: the forward pass is fine and the
    backward pass silently returns a gradient of the wrong shape."""
    while g.ndim > len(shape):            # axes prepended by broadcasting
        g = g.sum(axis=0)
    for i, s in enumerate(shape):         # axes stretched from length 1
        if s == 1 and g.shape[i] != 1:
            g = g.sum(axis=i, keepdims=True)
    return g.reshape(shape)

class Tensor:
    """Tape node.  `_bw(g)` maps the output cotangent to a tuple of parent
    cotangents: it applies the local transposed Jacobian, i.e. one VJP."""
    __slots__ = ("data", "grad", "_parents", "_bw", "_op", "_id")

    def __init__(self, data, parents=(), bw=None, op="leaf"):
        self.data = np.asarray(data, dtype=np.float64)
        self.grad, self._parents, self._bw, self._op = None, parents, bw, op
        _COUNTER[0] += 1
        self._id = _COUNTER[0]

    @property
    def shape(self):
        return self.data.shape

    def _w(self, x):
        return x if isinstance(x, Tensor) else Tensor(x)

    # ---- primitives: forward value, parents, and the local VJP ----
    def __add__(self, other):
        o = self._w(other)
        sa, sb = self.shape, o.shape
        return Tensor(self.data + o.data, (self, o),
                      lambda g: (_unbroadcast(g, sa), _unbroadcast(g, sb)), "+")

    def __mul__(self, other):
        o = self._w(other)
        sa, sb, a, b = self.shape, o.shape, self.data, o.data
        return Tensor(a * b, (self, o),
                      lambda g: (_unbroadcast(g * b, sa),
                                 _unbroadcast(g * a, sb)), "*")

    def __matmul__(self, other):
        o = self._w(other)
        a, b = self.data, o.data
        return Tensor(a @ b, (self, o), lambda g: (g @ b.T, a.T @ g), "@")

    def relu(self):
        m = (self.data > 0.0).astype(np.float64)   # subgradient 0 at exactly 0
        return Tensor(self.data * m, (self,), lambda g: (g * m,), "relu")

    def exp(self):
        e = np.exp(self.data)
        return Tensor(e, (self,), lambda g: (g * e,), "exp")

    def log(self):
        a = self.data
        return Tensor(np.log(a), (self,), lambda g: (g / a,), "log")

    def sum(self, axis=None, keepdims=False):
        shp, ax, kd = self.shape, axis, keepdims
        def bw(g):                        # transpose of "sum" is "broadcast"
            g = np.asarray(g)
            if ax is not None and not kd:
                g = np.expand_dims(g, ax)
            return (np.broadcast_to(g, shp).copy(),)
        return Tensor(self.data.sum(axis=ax, keepdims=kd), (self,), bw, "sum")

    __radd__, __rmul__ = __add__, __mul__
    def __neg__(self):
        return self * -1.0
    def __sub__(self, other):
        return self + (-self._w(other))
    def __truediv__(self, c):
        return self * (1.0 / float(c))

    def backward(self):
        """One reverse sweep: seed the output cotangent with 1, then apply each
        node's VJP once, in reverse creation order, accumulating at every leaf."""
        assert self.data.size == 1, "backward() must start from a scalar"
        seen, nodes, stack = set(), [], [self]
        while stack:                             # reachable sub-tape only
            n = stack.pop()
            if id(n) in seen:
                continue
            seen.add(id(n)); nodes.append(n); stack.extend(n._parents)
        nodes.sort(key=lambda t: -t._id)
        for n in nodes:
            n.grad = np.zeros_like(n.data)
        self.grad = np.ones_like(self.data)
        for n in nodes:
            if n._bw is None:
                continue
            for p, gp in zip(n._parents, n._bw(n.grad)):
                p.grad = p.grad + gp             # fan-out => sum of paths

def log_softmax(z):
    """Stable log-softmax over axis 1, built only from the primitives above.
    The max shift is a CONSTANT: log-softmax is shift-invariant, so no true
    derivative flows through the max and detaching it is exact, not a hack."""
    zs = z + Tensor(-z.data.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return zs - zs.exp().sum(axis=1, keepdims=True).log()

# ---- (a) random computation graphs vs central finite differences ----
SHAPES = [(3, 4), (4,), (1, 4), (3, 1), ()]      # every shape broadcasts to (3,4)

def random_graph(seed, leaves):
    """Random DAG over `leaves`; `seed` fixes the STRUCTURE so the identical
    function is rebuilt for every finite-difference probe."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    pool, mat = list(leaves[:-1]), leaves[-1]    # mat: the (4,4) matmul partner
    for _ in range(12):
        k = rng.integers(0, 6)
        a, b = pool[rng.integers(0, len(pool))], pool[rng.integers(0, len(pool))]
        if k == 0:   pool.append(a + b)
        elif k == 1: pool.append(a * b)
        elif k == 2: pool.append((a + b).relu())
        elif k == 3: pool.append((a * 0.25).exp())
        elif k == 4: pool.append((a * a + 1.0).log())      # argument > 0 always
        else:        pool.append((a + Tensor(np.zeros((3, 4)))) @ mat)
    # summing the leaves too guarantees each is reachable from the output
    return sum(p.sum() for p in pool) + mat.sum()

def fd_check(n_graphs=60, probes=3, eps=1e-5):
    """Each probed partial against (f(x+eps)-f(x-eps))/2eps, error O(eps^2)."""
    worst, n = 0.0, 0
    for trial in range(n_graphs):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(trial)
        data = [np.array(rng.normal(size=s) * 0.5) for s in SHAPES]
        data.append(rng.normal(size=(4, 4)) * 0.3)
        def f(vals):                     # same graph, fresh leaves
            return random_graph(1000 + trial, [Tensor(v.copy()) for v in vals])
        leaves = [Tensor(v.copy()) for v in data]
        random_graph(1000 + trial, leaves).backward()      # ONE reverse sweep
        for li in range(len(data)):
            for _ in range(probes):
                idx = tuple(rng.integers(0, d) for d in data[li].shape)
                base = float(data[li][idx])
                data[li][idx] = base + eps; fp = float(f(data).data)
                data[li][idx] = base - eps; fm = float(f(data).data)
                data[li][idx] = base
                num, ana = (fp - fm) / (2 * eps), float(leaves[li].grad[idx])
                worst = max(worst, abs(num - ana) / max(1.0, abs(num)))
                n += 1
    return worst, n

# ---- the 3-layer MLP used by checks (b) and (c) ----
def init_mlp(rng, d_in=2, h=32, d_out=2):
    ps = []
    for (a, b) in [(d_in, h), (h, h), (h, d_out)]:
        ps += [Tensor(rng.normal(size=(a, b)) * np.sqrt(2.0 / a)),
               Tensor(np.zeros((1, b)))]         # bias relies on broadcasting
    return ps

def mlp_loss(ps, X, Y):
    W1, b1, W2, b2, W3, b3 = ps
    h1 = (X @ W1 + b1).relu()                    # (B,h) + (1,h) broadcasts
    h2 = (h1 @ W2 + b2).relu()
    return -(Y * log_softmax(h2 @ W3 + b3)).sum() / X.shape[0]

def two_moons(n, rng, noise=0.18):
    t = rng.uniform(0.0, np.pi, size=n // 2)
    X = np.concatenate([np.stack([np.cos(t), np.sin(t)], 1),
                        np.stack([1.0 - np.cos(t), 0.5 - np.sin(t)], 1)])
    X = X + rng.normal(size=(n, 2)) * noise
    return X, np.concatenate([np.zeros(n // 2, int), np.ones(n // 2, int)])

if __name__ == "__main__":
    t0 = time.time()
    print("(a) random graphs vs central finite differences")
    worst, nprobe = fd_check()
    print(f"    60 graphs, {nprobe} probed partials: max rel error = {worst:.3e}")
    assert worst < 1e-6, worst

    print("(b) 3-layer MLP vs torch.autograd")
    X, y = two_moons(600, np.random.default_rng(1))
    Y = np.eye(2)[y]
    ps = init_mlp(np.random.default_rng(0))
    loss = mlp_loss(ps, Tensor(X), Tensor(Y))
    loss.backward()
    if torch is None:
        print("    [skipped: torch not installed]")
    else:
        tp = [torch.tensor(p.data, requires_grad=True) for p in ps]
        W1, b1, W2, b2, W3, b3 = tp
        h1 = torch.relu(torch.tensor(X) @ W1 + b1)
        h2 = torch.relu(h1 @ W2 + b2)
        tl = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(h2 @ W3 + b3, torch.tensor(y))
        tl.backward()
        dl = abs(float(tl.detach()) - float(loss.data))
        errs = [float(np.abs(p.grad - q.grad.numpy()).max()) for p, q in zip(ps, tp)]
        print(f"    loss ours={float(loss.data):.10f} torch={float(tl.detach()):.10f}"
              f"  |diff|={dl:.2e}")
        print("    max |grad diff|: " + "  ".join(
            f"{k}={e:.2e}" for k, e in zip("W1 b1 W2 b2 W3 b3".split(), errs)))
        assert dl < 1e-9 and max(errs) < 1e-6, (dl, errs)

    print("(c) training two-moons with this engine only")
    ps = init_mlp(np.random.default_rng(2))
    vel = [np.zeros_like(p.data) for p in ps]
    tX, tY, acc = Tensor(X), Tensor(Y), 0.0
    for ep in range(1, 601):                     # full-batch SGD + momentum
        loss = mlp_loss(ps, tX, tY)
        loss.backward()
        for p, v in zip(ps, vel):
            v *= 0.9
            v -= 0.5 * p.grad
            p.data += v
        if ep % 200 == 0:
            W1, b1, W2, b2, W3, b3 = ps
            h1 = np.maximum(X @ W1.data + b1.data, 0)
            h2 = np.maximum(h1 @ W2.data + b2.data, 0)
            acc = ((h2 @ W3.data + b3.data).argmax(1) == y).mean()
            print(f"    epoch {ep:4d}  loss {float(loss.data):.6f}"
                  f"  train acc {acc*100:.2f}%")
    assert acc > 0.99, acc
    print(f"done in {time.time()-t0:.1f}s")

Running it prints a maximum relative error of 1.145e-09 over 1,080 probed partials on 60 random graphs — the $u^{2/3}$ floor, so the finite differences are the inaccurate party, not the engine. Against torch.autograd the losses agree to $4.4\times10^{-16}$ and the six parameter gradients to between $8.3\times10^{-17}$ and $7.8\times10^{-16}$: the last bit of float64, not merely the $10^{-6}$ asked for. Training reaches 99.33% accuracy with loss 0.020490 at epoch 600, 1.5 seconds total.

Key Takeaways
  • Reverse-mode AD computes the entire gradient at a cost independent of the parameter count — one forward plus one reverse sweep, measured here at $3.15\times$ the forward pass — whereas finite differences cost $2N$ evaluations and forward-mode AD costs $N$ passes; at $N = 10^{11}$ that is the difference between a training step and an impossibility. Certain
  • Forward mode computes $Jv$ and needs one pass per input; reverse mode computes $J^\top w$ and needs one pass per output. Choose by the aspect ratio of the Jacobian: losses have one output and $10^{11}$ inputs, which is the maximally favorable case for reverse mode and the maximally unfavorable one for forward. Certain
  • Reverse mode's cost is memory: the local Jacobians depend on forward values, so the trace must be retained. The $\sqrt{L}$ checkpointing schedule cuts peak activation memory from $L$ to $2\sqrt{L}$ — $15.8\times$ at $L=1000$ — for exactly one extra forward pass, a $33\%$ compute overhead; $O(\log L)$ schedules exist but rarely pay in practice. Likely
  • Broadcasting is a linear copy map whose transpose is a sum, so a gradient flowing back through a broadcast must be reduced over the broadcast axes; getting this wrong is the defining bug of hand-written engines and can be silent rather than loud when the update is written out of place. Certain
  • At non-differentiable points every engine picks a subgradient by convention ($\mathrm{relu}'(0)=0$ in PyTorch 2.13 and here), and the convention can be wrong even where the composite function is differentiable — $\mathrm{relu}(x)-\mathrm{relu}(-x)$ is the identity, yet AD reports derivative $0$ at the origin. It has never been shown to matter for training; NaN-producing derivatives such as $\sqrt{\cdot}$ at $0$ demonstrably do. Contested
